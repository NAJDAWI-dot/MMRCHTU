Where this page and the printed rulebook ever disagree, the rulebook is the authority and the
judges' reading of it is final.

## The objective

Build an autonomous mouse that drives itself from a corner of a maze to the four cells at its
centre. You are not only trying to be quick. The score divides your successful runs by your
fastest one, so finishing often and finishing fast are both being rewarded — and neither wins
on its own.

## Eligibility

MMRC 26 is open to currently enrolled university students. An entry may be one person's work
or a team's, up to three members. Each team may enter at most one micromouse.

If the day allows time for it, teams give a short talk on their mouse before the contest —
five minutes at the outside.

## The maze

The competition maze is a 10×10 grid of 18cm cells with 12mm-thick walls, built to IEEE
micromouse standard dimensions. Measured from the inside faces of the walls, one cell is
18cm square. Allow 5% tolerance. The four corners of every cell meet at a 1.2cm lattice post,
and at least one wall is attached to every post in the maze.

The start is one of the four corners, walled on three sides. The start line sits between the
first cell and the next one clockwise — your clock starts as the mouse leaves the corner, not
when you release it. The goal is the block of four cells at the centre, and it has exactly one
entrance.

**The centre is an island.** Its walls touch nothing else in the maze, which means a mouse that
navigates by following one continuous wall will never arrive. It will circle the outer wall
until the time runs out. Expect several routes to the centre, and expect none of them to be
found by wall-hugging.

Do not make assumptions about what your sensors will see. The wall sides are white, the wall
tops are red and the floor is black, but the maze is painted plywood and the paint fades — it
will not be uniformly any of those things. The floor is slick, with no promised amount of
friction. Room lighting, temperature and humidity are whatever the venue happens to be on the
day: sunlight, fluorescent, incandescent, or some mix you did not plan for.

The layout is not published in advance. Every maze below is a fresh one, generated the moment
you loaded this page — press **New maze** to see another.

## The robot

- **Self-contained.** Onboard battery, onboard logic. No remote control, no external
  processing, no wireless data tether.
- **No combustion.** The energy source may not burn anything.
- **A footprint no larger than 25cm × 25cm.** Neither length nor width may exceed 25cm at any
  point during the entire match. If your mouse unfolds or extends, the expanded shape has to
  fit too. Height is unrestricted.
- **Nothing left behind.** No part of the mouse may detach in the maze.
- **Nothing done to the walls.** No jumping over, flying over or climbing them; no scratching,
  cutting, burning, marking, damaging or destroying them.

Any violation of these is immediate disqualification.

## The match

Each team gets **8 minutes** of access to the maze, starting the moment the contest
administrator grants it. That window is continuous. The clock does not stop between runs, and
every second spent adjusting the mouse, swapping a battery or restarting comes out of the same
8 minutes as the runs themselves.

Inside that window you may make as many runs as you can fit. Each one starts from the start
square. A run that reaches the centre is given a run time, and the shortest of them is your
**official time**.

- You may abort a run at any point.
- Touching the mouse during a run aborts it, and the mouse must then be taken off the maze.
- Once the mouse has crossed the finish line you may pick it up whenever you like — that run's
  time is already recorded, and removing it does not affect the time.

Once the maze has been revealed, you may not feed information about it into the mouse. Changing
switch positions is allowed; loading a map is not. Every team's source code is collected and
reviewed by the judges to confirm it.

## Search runs and speed runs

Nothing in the rules requires you to explore before you race. Nothing lets you skip it either: a
mouse can only compute a route through the cells it has actually mapped, so the first run is
spent finding out where the walls are and the later ones spend what it learned.

That is the trade the whole event turns on, and it is worth seeing rather than reading — a
thorough search costs time you cannot get back, and a hasty one leaves you racing through a map
with holes in it.

## Scoring

Both phases rank teams by the same formula:

> **Final score = (total successful runs ÷ official time) × 1000**

The higher score ranks higher. The rulebook's own worked example is the one to hold on to: a
team with four runs and a best lap of 25 seconds scores 160.0, while a team with one run and a
best lap of 18 seconds scores 55.6. The second mouse is quicker. It still places lower.

(The printed rulebook rounds that second figure down to 55.5. It is 1000 ÷ 18 = 55.55…, so the
site shows 55.6. Nothing about the ranking changes either way.)

**If nobody reaches the centre.** Every mouse that enters the centre within its 8 minutes ranks
above every mouse that does not. Those that never made it are ranked against each other by
their shortest traversable path — the most efficient wall-avoiding route towards the centre —
and the lowest value ranks highest.

## The tournament

**Check-in closes at 09:00.** The running order is then drawn at random by the organisers'
software, which also assigns each team its judging slot. Miss the slot you are given and you
forfeit your Phase 1 run, and with it the competition.

**Phase 1 — the qualifiers.** Every registered team runs in turn on one standard maze, each with
the full 8-minute window, each scored by the formula above.

**Phase 2 — the knockout league.** The teams that advance are seeded into a single-elimination
bracket. Two teams run at once on identical side-by-side mazes, sharing one 8-minute clock. The
higher score in each pairing goes through.

## Awards

- **1st place, MMRC26 Champion** — winner of the Grand Final.
- **2nd place** — runner-up in the Grand Final.
- **3rd place** — winner of the semi-final runners-up bracket.
- **Overall Highest Score** — the single best score posted in any Phase 2 match.
- **Best Code** — the most elegant logic, algorithmic efficiency or mapping technique, judged
  during the source code review.
- **Best Creative Design** — the most exceptional physical build: hardware solutions, custom PCB
  work, chassis geometry.

## Conduct

Unsportsmanlike conduct, maze tampering, or rule violations may result in disqualification at
the judges' discretion. Judges' decisions are final.

## Terminology

The words the judges will use, and what they mean here.

- **Micromouse** — the autonomous robot your team built.
- **Cell** — one 18cm square of navigable maze.
- **Lattice point (post)** — the 1.2cm square where four cells meet and walls connect.
- **Start square** — the corner cell, walled on three sides, that every run begins in.
- **Destination zone** — the four cells at the centre. One entrance.
- **Start line / finish line** — the invisible thresholds that start and stop the clock.
- **Match** — your continuous 8-minute window at the maze.
- **Run** — one attempt from the start square to the centre.
- **Official time** — the shortest run time you record during the match.
- **Touch** — any physical contact with the mouse after a run has started. It ends the run.
- **Abort** — ending a run deliberately, usually to rescue a stuck mouse.
- **Island goal** — a centre whose walls are detached from the rest of the maze. Ours is one.
- **Wall-hugging** — following one continuous wall. Against an island goal, an infinite loop.
- **Search run** — an exploring run that maps walls.
- **Speed run** — a later run that races the map the search built.
- **Shortest traversable path** — the most efficient wall-avoiding route to the centre, used to
  rank mice that never got there.
- **Simply connected maze** — a maze with no closed loops and no isolated walls.
